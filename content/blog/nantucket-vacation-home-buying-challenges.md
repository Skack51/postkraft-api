# 7 Nantucket Vacation Home Buying Challenges

Most people who buy on Nantucket have already bought real estate before. They have closed on a primary residence, maybe a ski house, maybe an investment property or two. Then they start looking here and discover that almost none of that experience transfers.

Nantucket vacation real estate operates on its own rules — a private listing culture, a 2% fee the buyer pays at closing, a commission that decides the color of your shutters, and a wastewater regulation that can attach a six-figure obligation to a deed at the moment of transfer. None of it is hidden. It is just local, and off-island buyers usually meet it in the wrong order: after they have fallen for a house.

Here are the seven obstacles that actually derail Nantucket purchases, and what to do about each one before you are under agreement.

## 1. The inventory is shrinking faster than the buyer pool

The defining feature of the Nantucket real estate market right now is not price. It is scarcity.

Through the first half of 2026, 89 homes sold on the island, down 19% from the 110 that traded in the same stretch of 2025. Inventory fell harder: the island closed June with 167 properties on the market against 245 a year earlier, a 32% drop. New listings thinned too — 52 came on in June versus 68 the previous June.

Yet total sales volume was $400.99 million, off just 4% from $418.87 million. Far fewer trades, nearly the same money. The median price rose about 11%, from $3,300,000 to $3,675,000.

That combination is what buyers feel as frustration. It is not a slow market or a soft market. It is a thin one, where fewer houses change hands at higher prices, and where the same handful of qualified buyers circle each new listing.

The scarcity is also structural rather than cyclical. Roughly half of Nantucket is permanently protected conservation land held by the Land Bank, the Nantucket Conservation Foundation, and the Land Council. That land is never coming to market. The island cannot add supply the way a mainland market can, which is why waiting for "more inventory next season" has been a losing strategy for two decades.

**What to do:** Stop calibrating to your home market's rhythm. Get financially ready — proof of funds, lender pre-underwriting, an attorney retained — before you find the house, not after. On Nantucket, readiness is the thing that gets you the house.

## 2. The best properties are sold before the public ever sees them

This is the single biggest gap between how off-island buyers search and how Nantucket actually trades.

The island runs its own multiple listing service. LINK Nantucket, operated by the Nantucket Association of Real Estate Brokers, is the local system of record, and it does syndicate to Zillow, Realtor.com, and other national portals. So the public feeds are real — they are just the last stop, not the first.

A meaningful share of the island's best homes never gets that far. Off-market Nantucket properties move through office exclusives, broker-to-broker calls, and whisper listings shared by text and in private channels with no formal record until a contract is signed. Sellers here have real reasons to prefer that: privacy, avoiding the appearance of a stale listing, and — in a market this thin — the knowledge that a qualified pool of buyers can be assembled without ever going public.

The practical consequence is that a buyer whose entire property search runs through national portals is shopping a filtered, delayed subset of the market and competing hardest for the properties everyone else can also see.

**What to do:** Engage a Nantucket-based broker early and be specific about what you want and what you will pay. Off-market access is not a subscription you buy; it is a function of someone credible being able to say "I have a buyer for this" when a colleague mentions a house over coffee in March. Vague buyers do not get those calls. Serious, well-defined ones do.

## 3. The Land Bank fee and the closing math that surprises people

Massachusetts buyers are used to the seller carrying transfer costs. Nantucket inverts that.

The Nantucket Islands Land Bank collects a 2% fee on real estate transfers, and **the buyer pays it.** On a $3.675 million purchase — this year's median — that is $73,500, due at closing, on top of everything else. It funds the conservation land that makes the island what it is, which is a fair trade, but it needs to be in your number from the first conversation.

There are statutory exemptions. The first-time homebuyer exemption shields up to $1,400,000 of the purchase price as of January 2026, and there is an affordable-housing exemption. Both carry five-year ownership and domicile requirements enforced by a lien recorded at the Nantucket County Registry of Deeds. Neither is available to a second-home buyer. If you are reading this as an off-island buyer, assume you pay the full 2%.

Financing is the second half of the problem. Purchases at these prices are jumbo loans, and appraisals are genuinely difficult when a market produces this few comparable sales — an appraiser working with thin, dated comps can come in low on a price that is entirely defensible to anyone who watched the bidding. Cash buyers are common here, and a financed offer competing against cash needs to be structured to compensate: shorter contingency periods, larger deposits, a lender who has closed on Nantucket before and will not be surprised by an island appraisal.

**What to do:** Build your true all-in number early — purchase price, 2% Land Bank fee, legal, inspections, insurance binder, and any deferred capital work the inspection turns up. Then decide what you can bid, rather than discovering the gap during a deal.

## 4. Zoning and the Historic District Commission decide what you can build

Nantucket is a National Historic Landmark District, and the Historic District Commission has authority over exterior architectural features across the island — not just the cobblestone core. New construction, additions, dormers, windows, siding, and paint colors visible from the outside all run through it.

Before a building permit application, any project with exterior impact needs an HDC Certificate of Appropriateness. That is a legal prerequisite, not a formality. The certificate is valid for three years once issued, and the realistic pre-construction timeline for an exterior project is three to four months or more, with another two to three weeks for the building permit after HDC approval.

Zoning sets the other boundary. Ground cover ratio caps how much of your lot can be built on by district, and it is the number that quietly kills renovation plans. Buyers routinely fall for a modest house on a good lot with a plan to double it, then learn the lot has little or no ground cover remaining.

Then there is cost. Construction on Nantucket runs from roughly $450 per square foot for modular up to $700 per square foot and higher for stick-built, before the island premium works through the schedule — every board, appliance, and crew arrives by ferry, freight and vehicle reservations compete with peak-season traffic, and good contractors book out far in advance.

**What to do:** Before you go under agreement on a house you intend to change, have a local architect or builder confirm the remaining ground cover and pressure-test the plan against HDC precedent. "We'll renovate later" is an assumption that needs to be verified during diligence, not after closing.

## 5. Septic, water, and the obligations that transfer with the deed

Much of Nantucket is not on municipal sewer, and the island's wastewater rules are stricter than the Massachusetts Title 5 baseline that governs the rest of the state.

The rule most likely to affect your purchase: properties inside the Nantucket Harbor Watershed Protection District that are not connected to municipal sewer must install a DEP-approved nitrogen-reducing Innovative/Alternative system — and **transfer of the property is one of the triggers.** So is new construction, a building permit that adds living space, a system replacement or expansion, and a failure. Conventional systems in Zones A and B of the district also carry a five-year inspection cycle with reports filed to the Health Department. New construction in nitrogen-sensitive areas is capped at 110 gallons of design flow per system.

The town is under a state mandate through the Massachusetts Estuaries Program to reduce nitrogen loading in Nantucket Harbor, Madaket Harbor, Long Pond, Sesachacha Pond, and potentially other embayments, so the direction of travel on these regulations is toward more stringent, not less.

None of this makes a house a bad buy. An IA system upgrade is a known, quotable cost. The failure mode is discovering it late — when it becomes a rushed negotiation two weeks before a closing you have already scheduled movers around.

**What to do:** Establish early whether the property is on sewer or septic, whether it sits in the Harbor Watershed Protection District, what type of system it has, and when it was last inspected. If an IA upgrade will be triggered at transfer, get a real quote during diligence and put it in your offer math.

## 6. Coastal risk is real, local, and not evenly priced

Erosion on Nantucket is not an abstraction about the year 2100. It is a street.

Along the Sconset bluff, the top of the bluff sits as little as 29 feet from Baxter Road at its closest point, with many homes that close or closer. Eight houses have been moved or demolished, leaving vacant lots, and twelve more have been relocated back toward the road on their own lots to buy time. Documented losses reached 30 to 40 feet in some locations during a single storm season in 2012–2013.

That is the most dramatic stretch, but the underlying issue is islandwide: FEMA flood zone designations, non-regulatory coastal erosion hazard mapping, flood insurance availability and cost, and — increasingly — whether a carrier will write the policy at all. Insurability now affects lending, which affects resale, which affects value. Two houses a quarter mile apart can carry meaningfully different risk profiles and meaningfully different long-term carrying costs.

Luxury vacation homes on the water command the premium precisely because of where they sit. That is the trade, and plenty of buyers make it knowingly. The mistake is making it unknowingly.

**What to do:** Pull the FEMA flood map and coastal erosion hazard mapping for the specific parcel, get an insurance quote during diligence rather than after, and ask directly about historical erosion rates for that stretch of shoreline. On a waterfront purchase, treat insurance as a diligence item with the same weight as the inspection.

## 7. Your rental-income math rests on rules that just changed

If your purchase assumes rental income, know that Nantucket spent five years in an unresolved fight over short-term rentals — and that it resolved recently enough that plenty of published advice is still wrong.

The short version. A Massachusetts Land Court ruling held that short-term renting was not an allowed use in the Residential Old Historic district unless the owner lived in the home and rented rooms rather than the whole house. Town Meeting then failed six consecutive times to settle the zoning question; all four STR proposals went down at the May 2025 Annual Town Meeting. At a Special Town Meeting on November 4, 2025, Article 1 finally passed 1,045 to 421 — about 71%, clearing the two-thirds threshold — codifying short-term rentals as an allowed principal use islandwide. A legal challenge asked the Attorney General to disapprove it; in May 2026 the AG upheld the bylaw.

So STRs are permitted islandwide today. Two caveats matter for anyone underwriting rental income. First, separate from zoning, Nantucket has required Board of Health short-term rental registration since January 1, 2023 — you need a certificate of registration to operate, and that is an ongoing compliance obligation, not a one-time box. Second, this issue has been contested at Town Meeting every year for half a decade; a resolved question is not necessarily a permanently closed one.

**What to do:** Underwrite the property so it works on the fundamentals, with rental income as upside rather than the load-bearing assumption. Confirm registration requirements and any HOA or covenant restrictions on the specific property — private restrictions can be tighter than town zoning, and they do not change when Town Meeting votes.

## The pattern behind all seven

Every one of these obstacles is knowable in advance. None of them is a reason not to buy on Nantucket. What they punish is sequence — buyers who fall for a house first and investigate the island's rules second.

The buyers who do well here reverse that order. They get their financing and their team assembled before they are emotionally committed. They build the Land Bank fee and likely capital work into their number from the start. They establish what a property can become — ground cover, HDC precedent, septic obligations, insurability — while they still have the leverage to walk away. And they build a relationship with a local broker early enough that they hear about the right house in March instead of reading about the sale in August.

That is what separates a serious property search on Nantucket from a frustrating one.
